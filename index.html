Research CV (PDF, opens in a new tab)

I am a PhD student in Economics at Stockholm University. My research interests are in political economy, economic development, and labor. I am on the 2026–27 economics job market.

I am interested in how political frictions affect economic development and growth. My job market paper studies how political influence changes bureaucratic priorities and affects the regulatory environment for private investors. In closely related work, I document how career incentives in India induce bureaucrats to avoid postings in districts that have higher need for welfare and governance.

Prior to graduate school, I got my Master’s degree from the University of Chicago and then worked as a research assistant with Marshall Burke at the Doerr School of Sustainability at Stanford University.

Job Market Paper

Political Influence, Bureaucratic Priorities, and Regulatory Process in India

Abstract: Political Influence, Bureaucratic Priorities, and Regulatory Process in India
Draft (PDF on Dropbox, opens in a new tab) Slides (PDF, opens in a new tab) Media: Ideas of India, Mercatus Center (opens in a new tab)

Does political influence over bureaucracy make it more productive, or merely reorder its priorities? Using a novel data source with the life cycle of large investment projects, I build a panel that captures regulatory processing for government and private projects. Using party alignment between the local politician and the state ruling party as a measure of the politician’s influence, I design two empirical designs to test the effect of political influence: an event study and a close-election RD. Both designs give consistent results that tell me that politicians use their influence to capture bureaucratic attention, changing the composition of bureaucratic output but not the total output. When political influence is high, bureaucrats shift their output towards government projects that come at the cost of private projects, and the share of private projects in the output goes down by 16 pp, roughly 41% of the baseline. The one-period success rate of private projects waiting in the queue also goes down by around 6 pp, roughly half of the baseline. Outcomes for government projects improve by a similar amount. Evidence suggests that bureaucrats give higher priority to influential politicians because of career returns. A back-of-the-envelope calculation implies about 4 months of additional delay per private project and 0.29 million private jobs deferred, caused by political capture of bureaucracy. Regulatory reforms are common prescriptions to promote investment, but the paper argues that the political economy of regulatory implementation might also be important to improve the regulatory environment.

Political Allocation of State Capacity

Abstract: Political Allocation of State Capacity
Draft (PDF on Dropbox, opens in a new tab)

This paper studies how shifts in local political influence reshape the subnational allocation of the bureaucratic state, even when aggregate bureaucratic strength and formal institutions remain fixed. I develop a framework in which heterogeneity in politicians’ influence within state-level posting markets, combined with bureaucrats’ career incentives, endogenously reallocates bureaucratic capacity across space. Using India’s 2008 electoral delimitation, which quasi-randomly altered districts’ exposure to reserved caste politicians, I show that districts newly exposed to reserved constituencies experience a sharp and persistent decline in the flow of incoming bureaucrats, leading to a contraction in local state capacity (size effect) and systematic changes in officer type (composition effect). These effects are driven by the caste identity of the elected politicians rather than political turnover per se. I formalize two distinct channels that generate these effects: assortative matching and career concerns of bureaucrats and find evidence for both. More broadly, the paper documents an unintended effect of a political reform: policy aimed at increasing political representation also caused a decrease in the local state capacity.

Police Funding Inequality and Police Performance in the US

with Mitch Downey and Ben Grunwald

Abstract: Police Funding Inequality and Police Performance in the US

Police departments in the USA draw the majority of their funding from revenues generated by local governments. This means that funding for local police can be unequal within a metropolitan area among neighbouring cities. What are the consequences of this arrangement for the allocation of police officers, the quality of police representation in majority-minority communities, and aggregate crime and officer-involved violence? Using a shift-share instrument, we estimate the effects of increasing income inequality in MSAs with high levels of fiscal fragmentation (i.e., instances where the population is divided across many different legally and fiscally independent cities), both relative to increasing inequality when fragmentation is low, and relative to highly fragmented MSAs that do not see increasing inequality.

Can the State Choose Winners? Evidence from Startup Promotion in India

with Sai Zhang (USC)

Abstract: Can the State Choose Winners? Evidence from Startup Promotion in India

In 2016, the Indian government rolled out Startup India to provide a number of incentives to promote the startup ecosystem. This bundle of treatment policies included regulatory concessions, priority access to public procurement contracts, and selection into various incubation centers. Using a shift-share design, we document that (i) the policy increased new firm registration, and (ii) the policy screened on firms already doing better than control firms, but the treated firms did no better than control firms once selected, using employee headcount as a measure. Treated firms apply for more public procurement contracts than control firms. Preliminary findings imply that the policy induced firms to divert more attention towards applying for public contracts rather than innovation or competing in the product market.
