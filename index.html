Job market paper

Political Influence, Bureaucratic Priorities, and Capital Accumulation in India

I study how political influence shapes bureaucratic effort, as measured by the rate of regulatory clearances of large capital-investment projects. Using party alignment between the local member of parliament (MP) and the state government to isolate the MP’s influence, I find that an exogenous change in influence does not have a uniform level effect on bureaucratic effort. Rather, bureaucrats respond to the change by reallocating effort across competing tasks. In other words, regulatory experience of one kind of project improves at the cost of other. Two independent sources of variation in the local MP’s influence motivate two separate research designs. The event study design uses state elections that do not affect the local MP, and a close-election RD uses national parliament elections that do not change the state government. Both designs recover the same reallocation, running in opposite directions. Across the identifying comparisons, no statute or procedure changes, so the estimates operate through the MP’s influence rather than the written rules. When the local MP loses influence, effort on existing private projects rises by roughly 30%, while effort on central-government projects tied to the MP falls by a similar amount. The RD estimates show the mirror image: an MP who gains influence at a close election shifts effort toward central-government projects at the expense of private ones. The results describe a form of political capture that works through priority rather than rents. A severely personnel-constrained bureaucracy advances an influential politician’s agenda first. The cost is the effort withdrawn from other claimants on the same administrative system, even though every decision remains formally lawful. Because officials respond to career incentives rather than statute, the political configuration of the state belongs among the inputs that determine growth.

Work in progress

Political overreach, state capacity and firm dynamics

Abstract: Political overreach, state capacity and firm dynamics

Political overreach into bureaucracy is a routine occurrence in India. How does this affect bureaucrats’ ability and effectiveness? I investigate the effects of political overreach on local state capacity as well as on firm behaviour. Using the universe of bureaucratic postings and transfers and biographical data on state level politicians, I empirically document that politicians’ characteristics are associated with unusual transfer patterns. Using an instrumental variable that captures the shock to politicians’ willingness to overreach, I estimate the effect of political overreach on firms’ entry and investment behaviour.

The goal with this project is to generate quantitative evidence of growth effects of distortions arising out of political frictions.

Police funding inequality and police performance in US

with Mitch Downey

Abstract: Police funding inequality and police performance in US

Police departments in the USA draw the majority of their funding from revenues generated by local governments. This means that funding for local police can be unequal within a metropolitan area among neighbouring cities. What are the consequences of this arrangement for the allocation of police officers, the quality of police representation in majority-minority communities, and aggregate crime and officer-involved violence? Using a shift-share instrument, we estimate the effects of increasing income inequality in MSAs with high levels of fiscal fragmentation (i.e., instances where the population is divided across many different legally and fiscally independent cities), both relative to increasing inequality when fragmentation is low, and relative to highly fragmented MSAs that do not see increasing inequality.
